In _Ikiru_, Akira Kurosawa shows a bureaucracy that has become an end in itself. Its officials are not necessarily evil; they are something more troubling: men who have become so accustomed to the machinery of administration that they can no longer distinguish the purpose of their work from the procedures through which it is performed. 

When a group of mothers approaches the municipal government to request a children's playground, their petition is passed from one department to another, each office finding a reason why the matter belongs elsewhere. The government exists to serve the public, yet the public must navigate an obstacle course of government offices just to be heard with no hope.

Watanabe is a lifelong bureaucrat who discovers that he has terminal cancer and finally recognizes the emptiness of his existence. He had spent decades processing documents, but he realizes that he has accomplished almost nothing that he can point to as meaningful. Faced with his imminent death, he decides to devote what remains of his life to getting the playground built. It is a modest ambition, but within a system designed to perpetuate its own routines, even this becomes an extraordinary undertaking.

Then, just as Watanabe's struggle appears to have reached its conclusion, the film takes us to his funeral. The playground has been built, but the bureaucrats gathered there begin to dispute who deserves credit for it. Most are eager to explain the achievement through the ordinary functioning of their departments, minimizing Watanabe's. One official, however, insists that Watanabe was the decisive force behind the project. He recalls the man's persistence, the obstacles he overcame, and the personal sacrifices he made to accomplish what the entire administrative apparatus had failed to do on its own.

In the end, totally drunk with sake, finally the group broke up, promising the gods to rededicate their lives to “giving up the individual self for the public good.” as Watanabe himself had done.

The final frame of the film was all those fellows back at the office, leafing thru papers and affixing their seals as they always had done, once again oblivious to the people who came in to make requests.

It's still the system we live in today. It was as if Kurosawa saw a glimpse of the future, and captured it on a monochromatic film of 1950's.

Bureaucracy exists to serve the public, yet its officials have become so absorbed in procedures, hierarchies, and the preservation of routine that they can no longer see the people behind the paperwork.
