    Practicing OPSEC as an Individual

    17-10-2026

    What is OPSEC

    “If I am able to determine the enemy’s dispositions while at the same time I conceal my own, then I can concentrate and he must divide.”

    ~Sun Tzu, The Art of War 400–320 BC

    Women's Army Corps anti-rumor propaganda (1941–1945)

    Operational security is a process that identifies critical information to determine whether friendly actions can be observed by enemy intelligence, determines if information obtained by adversaries could be interpreted to be useful to them, and then executes selected measures that eliminate or reduce adversary exploitation of friendly critical information.

    It’s most important characteristic is that it is a capability that employs a process. OPSEC is not a collection of specific rules and instructions. It is an analytical, planning, and executional process that can be applied to any operation or activity for the purpose of denying critical information to an adversary.

    So while OPSEC began as practices for the US millitary to identify their adversaries and deny critical information to them, it has been gaining attention in the privacy communities of normal people who are tired of mass surveillance and surveillance capitalism economy.

    Practicing OPSEC

    My OPSEC model is rather extreme and difficult to replicate and live off therefore I highly recommend following this guide to achieve an acceptable level of privacy. The Android Defensive Security System

    In the “You are a commodity” section, we saw how much power tech companies have over us and how many aspects of our lives they can affect, directly or indirectly. So, how do we escape it?

    The only winning move is not to play.

    That really is it. It’s impossible to fight powerful tech companies with trillions of dollars at their disposal. But we can always opt out.

    It’s important to understand that true privacy is impossible to achieve. Everything we do can only reduce our digital footprint.

    Here, I briefly outline my OPSEC model. It works for me because I understand my threat model, know how to use these tools, and have adapted my behavior and lifestyle to be more privacy-conscious. Every individual has different circumstances and adversaries however here our adversaries appear to be rather common. It's the big tech companies trying to commodify us and extract as much information on us possible. I will not be sharing extremely sensitive insights as it will undermine my own OPSEC but I try to reveal it to an extent which can aid others shape their own models.

    The first step is to delete every social media service and other online service that you use. Otherwise there's not much point in going forward since the very foundations of our internet are built on a surveillance infrastructure.

    There exists a wide variety of open source and decentralized alternatives to social media and other servicea which can be adopted.

    You can find a massive curated list of services and alternatives that respect your privacy here

    Phone

    I find it genuinely amusing how people feel compelled to purchase the latest iPhone or flagship smartphone, often at prices approaching those of a car. An iPhone Duo 2TB maximum-storage variant priced at ₹4,49,900 (USD $5,112) in India is a striking example. Despite its extraordinary computational capabilities, many users primarily employ it for doomscrolling, watching short-form videos, messaging, and taking photographs.

    From a technical perspective, this shows the disconnect between computational capability and actual utilisation. Modern flagship smartphones feature powerful SoCs, NPUs, advanced image signal processors, and sophisticated computational photography systems. Yet routine activities such as scrolling social media barely exploit their full processing potential. Although this hardware can improve responsiveness and longevity, its marginal practical value does not matter because far inferior and cheaper hardware can also achieve all of it.

    The psychology behind this behaviour is equally interesting. Conspicuous consumption, a concept associated with economist Thorstein Veblen, describes purchasing goods partly to signal wealth and social status, a tendency increasingly visible in India’s status-conscious consumer culture. A flagship smartphone becomes more than a computing device; its branding, design, and recognisability serve as social signals. Consumers may therefore be paying not merely for technological capability, but for the prestige associated with owning it.

    The irony is that consumers often pay a premium for perceived privacy and security while accepting restrictions on software freedom and limited transparency into proprietary systems.

    My Phone

    My phone is my daily driver, and I’ve spent the most effort hardening it. As a matter of fact, installing custom roms and open source software have become more significant than ever given how Google is on the verge of killing sideloading.

    Hardware and software

    I have a Xiaomi Mi 10T Pro (2020), which I bought around five years ago. It still runs as well as new.

    It currently runs LineageOS 24.0-xxx-Nightly (Android 17), with the latest security patch as of October 11, 2026.

    LineageOS provides a recent, debloated Android build for many devices, but it still relies on some Google components and requires manual hardening.

    GrapheneOS is the best privacy-focused custom ROM for Android phones, in my opinion. However, it is available only for Google Pixel devices, which I don’t own. GrapheneOS offers unmatched security, while LineageOS can provide a similar level of privacy.

    I wouldn’t recommend LineageOS to someone facing a dangerous adversary; in that case, GrapheneOS would be the better choice for its security.

    Hardening tweaks

    Rooting

    I use Magisk to gain root access, which lets me modify system files and apply hardening measures. One disadvantage compared with KernelSU is that Magisk must be manually installed via ADB after each system update. This takes about two minutes, but Magisk has a large community and supports prebuilt modules.

    Debloating

    For some reason, LineageOS still includes a few Google apps, such as Google Assistant. These can be removed with the De-bloater module.

    Captive portal

    Android’s captive-portal check contacts Google when connecting to public Wi-Fi. It can be replaced with a privacy-respecting alternative, such as Kuketz, or disabled altogether. Disabling it may make some public Wi-Fi networks harder to connect to.

    To set Kuketz as the captive portal, run these commands over ADB:

    adb shell 'settings put global captive_portal_http_url "http://captiveportal.kuketz.de"'
adb shell 'settings put global captive_portal_https_url "https://captiveportal.kuketz.de"'
adb shell 'settings put global captive_portal_fallback_url "http://captiveportal.kuketz.de"'
adb shell 'settings put global captive_portal_other_fall_back_urls "http://captiveportal.kuketz.de"'

    To confirm the change, run:

    adb shell 'settings get global captive_portal_https_url'

    WebView

    The default WebView included with LineageOS is Chromium-based. It is open source, but includes some proprietary components maintained by Google. I replace it by installing the Open WebView module.

    SUPL and Google Fused Location Provider

    SUPL is used as part of the A-GPS (Assisted GPS) system to get a faster Time to First Fix. The problem is that Android's implementation automatically sends the IMSI (ID of the SIM card) to the SUPL provider for no apparent reason. And because Google is the default provider it's a big breach of privacy.

    SUPL replacer module replaces the SUPL provider with a proxy server provided by GrapheneOS. The non-profit open source project is known for its security and privacy involvement.

    Connectivity checks

    When connecting to Wi-Fi or a cellular network, the phone may contact Google to check connectivity. I block these domains by adding them to my hosts file:

    connectivitycheck.gstatic.com
connectivitycheck.android.com

    With those changes in place, the phone no longer relies on Google services for these functions. Next is the software side.

    OPSEC

    InviZible Pro is the backbone of my OPSEC setup. It combines DNSCrypt, Tor, and I2P in one app.

    The app runs in root mode, using a kernel-level AF+ firewall. It provides these services system-wide and includes a kill switch that blocks apps from connecting if the services go down. DNSCrypt queries are encrypted, with enforcement handled at the kernel level through iptables.

    I also enabled per-network MAC-address randomization in the phone’s settings.

    Browser

    WebLibre is my default browser. It is built on the Gecko engine and allows each website to run in its own isolated process. Profiles let me switch between Tor, I2P, and a direct connection.

    Extensions

    I use several extensions to improve privacy without compromising fingerprinting protection:

    • Dark Reader: Applies a dark theme to websites. I can’t do without it.
    • LibRedirect: Redirects links to popular services such as YouTube, Reddit, IMDb, and Pinterest to privacy-respecting front ends and back ends. This lets me browse some services without visiting their original sites or signing in.
    • Privacy Badger: Detects tracking domains and blocks them.
    • NoScript: Disables JavaScript by default and lets me allow it for individual websites. It also helps reduce exposure to cross-site scripting attacks.
    • LocalCDN: Blocks requests to external CDNs and serves some resources locally to reduce external requests.
    • uBlock Origin: The best ad blocker and should be used by everyone to surf the web.

    University

    My university requires me to use WhatsApp and sign in to its portal with a Google account assigned to students.

    I keep the Google account in an isolated Tor container. This lets me access the portal without using that account in my regular browsing environment.

    In India, WhatsApp is difficult to avoid, so I use it only for university matters.

    I run the WhatsApp app in an isolated Waydroid container on my Linux machine. On my phone, I use WhatsApp Web in the browser, routed through Tor. Custom extension rules help limit requests, and I use an extension to spoof a desktop user agent so the site works. WhatsApp can still collect metadata; messages are NOT end-to-end encrypted. I use the service strictly for university.

    My uBlock Origin rules for WhatsApp Web:

    ! WhatsApp Web
||dit.whatsapp.net/deidentified_telemetry$xhr,third-party
||crashlogs.whatsapp.net^
||flows.whatsapp.net^
||pps.whatsapp.net^
||webtp.whatsapp.net^
||graph.whatsapp.net^
||mmg.whatsapp.net^
||dit.whatsapp.net^
||static.whatsapp.net/rsrc.php/yS/r/T_xj3a3ApC9.webp^

    I originally tried to improve the privacy of the WhatsApp app by using XPL-EX to spoof system properties and a DNS blocker to block requests the app makes frequently. That approach wasn’t effective enough, so I concluded that the app should be used only in an isolated environment. It collects too much information for my comfort.

    The web app is easier for me to control through browser settings and extensions.

    You might wonder why I route WhatsApp through Tor when Meta already knows my identity. I do this because I use networks outside my home, such as university Wi-Fi, and I don’t want Meta to associate those connections with my actual location.

    Apps

    • Obtainium: Installs and manages apps directly from their sources.
    • CoMaps: A map app based on OpenStreetMap.
    • WebLibre: My primary hardened browser.
    • IronFox: My secondary hardened browser.
    • Tor Browser: The official Tor browser.
    • KeePassDX: An encrypted password manager and database.
    • LibreTorrent: A torrent client.
    • Metrolist: An open-source YouTube Music app, routed through Tor.
    • Obsidian: A proprietary note-taking app. I haven’t found a free, open-source alternative that works as well for me. I block it with my firewall.
    • Orion Viewer: A PDF and file viewer.
    • LibreOffice Viewer: A document viewer.
    • OSS Document Scanner: Scans documents and creates PDFs. I prefer it to Adobe Scan.
    • Poweramp: Best music player for android.
    • ServerBox: Connects to my server over SSH and helps me manage it.
    • Tailscale: Lets me connect to my server remotely.
    • Molly: A hardened Signal fork that’s easy to recommend because of its simplicity and privacy features.
    • SimpleX: My preferred instant-messaging app.
    • Usagi: A manga reader. I route it through Tor because it retrieves comics from various sources.
    • Windscribe: A VPN with a free plan that includes 15 GB of data per month.
    • ZArchiver: A proprietary file manager that I find easy to use. It doesn’t connect to the internet, but I block it with my firewall anyway.
    • KDE Connect: Connects my phone with my other Linux machines.

    Web apps and self-hosting

    I access other online services, such as email providers, through their websites. Their web versions offer an experience similar to their apps, and installing more apps adds complexity. Open source doesn’t automatically mean an app is free of trackers.

    I access other services I rely on, such as PDF tools and cloud storage, through my server.

    Cellular

    The Public Switched Telephone Network (PSTN), the dominant form of communication, is proprietary. So is the broadband chip in my phone. No one outside the manufacturers knows exactly what code it runs or what it can do.

    Cellular providers collect extensive information about how their networks are used. They can see whom you call and for how long; calls and SMS are not encrypted by the cellular network. Providers can also record timestamps and connections made over the network. Your location can be estimated through cell-tower triangulation.

    This information can be used to profile you, sold to data brokers, or shared with government agencies. Data brokers may also sell personal information to others.

    A YouTube video from 14 years ago, “Your phone company is watching” by Malte Spitz, illustrates the scale of the issue. Surveillance infrastructure has advanced considerably since then.

    My phone stays in airplane mode about 99% of the time. I don’t recharge my mobile number unless I need to, and I avoid giving it out. There are no affordable VoIP-number providers in India outside the commercial sector, as far as I know.

    I mostly rely on home Wi-Fi and connect to my university’s public Wi-Fi when I’m there. If I go elsewhere, I ask to use the available Wi-Fi. Between those networks, I’m disconnected. In an emergency, I can ask someone nearby to share a hotspot.

    I use Signal for calls, which lets me use my phone more like a landline.

    Without internet access or Google’s Fused Location Provider, my phone can take up to a minute to get a satellite fix. After that, the map app works smoothly.

    I also don’t use microG since Idon’t use proprietary apps that depend on Google Play services anymore, and microG’s notification services and other aspects tend to contact Google’s servers.

    Conclusion

    Ultimately, my ability to maintain a high degree of privacy and anonymity stems from learning to live without unnecessary dependence on invasive software and services. This approach is far from perfect and comes with its own practical limitations, but digital privacy is not merely a matter of choosing secure tools; it also requires making deliberate decisions about one's digital footprint, data exposure, and dependence on centralised platforms. The real challenge is balancing operational security (OPSEC) with the practical demands of participating in the society I live in, without compromising more of my autonomy than necessary.
